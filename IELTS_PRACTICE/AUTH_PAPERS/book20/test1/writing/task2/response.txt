The attainment and retention of human dignity should be the top priority of every social system, with the
right to life the foundation of this principle. Therefore, the necessity of access to clean water in
every region inhabited by humans seems undisputed. However, the proposal that every home should have free access
to potable water faces serious practical and economic challenges, which this essay will examine.

Unless people have constant access to clean water, their most basic physiological needs will not be met. This
means that even a short-term disruption in water supply could jeopardize lives and health. The human physiological
system heavily relies on clean water, as the function of its organs
could be permanently impaired without adequate water intake. In addition to the physiological
impairment, poor hygiene, another damaging consequence of water shortage, could lead to an outbreak of infectious
diseases, while water has been used as the cheapest means of sanitation for thousands of years. However, this does
not mean that every home should have free access to potable water.

In fact, there are costly stages preceding the provision of clean water for household use. At first, a water
source should be identified, then transferred to a reservoir where it is maintained and purified.
Then, it is distributed through a complicated network of pipelines to households. Hence, although access to clean
water is a basic human right, the infrastructure required-- sourcing, treatment, maintenance and distribution--
is costly, particularly in arid and remote areas. Consequently, service fees must be levied for this to ensure
its long-term sustainability.

Nevertheless, even if the local administration has enough financial resources and willingness to provide free
access to clean water, it  must be capped to a certain level. Implementing such a policy can encourage responsible consumption, promote conservation, and foster public awareness of water’s value as a finite resource.

In conclusion, even though access to clean water is intrinsically linked to the right to life, providing it free of
charge to every household is neither practical nor sustainable.
