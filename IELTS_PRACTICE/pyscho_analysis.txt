1. Self-Presentation

Definition:
Self-presentation refers to the process of managing how one is perceived by others in social or evaluative contexts. It is also called “impression management.”

Purpose:
Individuals present themselves strategically to obtain social rewards, avoid negative judgments, or maintain desirable identities.

Mechanisms:

selective disclosure of information

modulation of affect (tone, politeness, enthusiasm)

strategic adherence to norms

audience sensitivity and role-taking

conversational performance

Relevance to cognition & language:
Self-presentation involves both social cognition (what does the audience expect?) and behavioral execution (how do I act/speak?). In test contexts such as interviews or oral exams, it becomes especially salient.

Key disciplinary roots:
Sociology (Goffman), social psychology (Leary, Baumeister).

2. Self-Schema

Definition:
A self-schema is a cognitive structure (mental framework) that organizes knowledge about the self in particular domains (e.g., “I am sociable,” “I am artistic,” “I am shy”).

Function:
Self-schemata help individuals:

classify self-related information

make predictions about behavior

process self-relevant stimuli efficiently

maintain consistency of identity

Properties:

stable but modifiable

domain-specific (academic, social, emotional, professional)

influence memory encoding and retrieval

Relevance to cognition & memory:
Self-schemas facilitate faster processing of self-relevant information and bias recall toward experiences consistent with one’s self-views.

Disciplinary roots:
Cognitive and social psychology.

3. Self-Concept

Definition:
Self-concept is the broad, organized set of beliefs, attributes, and evaluations one holds about oneself. It encompasses both cognitive (knowledge) and affective (value) components.

Components often included:

academic self-concept

social self-concept

emotional self-concept

physical self-concept

moral identity

self-esteem (evaluative dimension)

Relation to self-schema:
Self-schema is considered the structural element; self-concept is the overarching, integrative representation of the self.

Relevance to performance & motivation:
Self-concept influences choice, persistence, confidence, and interactional behavior. In evaluative settings, it shapes willingness to speak, the level of detail provided, and conversational demeanor.

4. Autobiographical Memory Retrieval

Definition:
Autobiographical memory refers to memory for personal experiences (episodic) and personal facts (semantic) related to one’s life.

Two major components:

Episodic memory
Specific events situated in time and place (e.g., “When I moved to the city…”).

Semantic personal knowledge
Stable facts about oneself (e.g., hometown, school, preferences, habits).

Retrieval processes:

involuntary vs intentional recall

self-relevance filtering

narrative reconstruction

working memory involvement for verbal output

Cognitive role:
Autobiographical memory supports continuity of identity, narrative self, and personal meaning-making.

Relevance to speech tasks:
When individuals describe routines, preferences, or past events, they tap into autobiographical memory systems to generate coherent responses.

5. Self-Awareness

Definition:
Self-awareness is the ability to attend to and monitor internal states, traits, actions, and appearance in relation to personal standards and/or social evaluation.

Two common distinctions:

Private self-awareness
Focus on inner thoughts, feelings, preferences, and internal states.

Public self-awareness
Focus on the self as viewed by others (observable behavior, impression, social evaluation).

Functional consequences:

increases self-regulation

modulates anxiety in performance contexts

enhances error monitoring

alters expressive behaviors and speech patterns

Relevance to evaluative tasks:
Interview-style assessments (including oral proficiency tests) heighten public self-awareness, leading participants to monitor speech, politeness, correctness, and fluency more closely.

How These Constructs Interrelate

These constructs form a coherent theoretical network:

Autobiographical memory provides content for personal expression.

Self-schema structures how that content is organized.

Self-concept integrates that structure into identity.

Self-awareness monitors and regulates how identity is expressed.

Self-presentation operationalizes expression for a social audience.

1. Levelt’s Speech Production Model (1989, 1993)

Levelt’s model is foundational for psycholinguistic speech planning. It comprises three major components:

Conceptualizer

Generates preverbal message

Selects communicative intent and perspective

Activates event schema (narrative in Part 2)

Formulator

Grammatical encoding (syntactic/functional assignment)

Phonological encoding (syllabification, prosody)

Articulator

Motor execution of speech

IELTS Part 2 mapping:

Planning time (1 min) corresponds to conceptualizer stage with partial macro-planning

Speaking time triggers parallel processing of all three stages

Importantly: in L2, conceptualization is relatively intact, but formulation becomes resource-intensive due to lexical access and morphosyntax constraints.

2. Kormos’ L2 Speech Production Model (2006)

Kormos adapts Levelt specifically for second language performance, emphasizing:

reduced automaticity

greater working memory load

monitoring burden

lexical retrieval bottlenecks

code-switching tendencies

strategic planning

For Part 2:

monologic extended speech increases formulation demands

reliance on autobiographical content reduces conceptual complexity

time pressure prevents deep monitoring, forcing strategic simplification

Kormos predicts that L2 speakers use:

narrative templates

formulaic language

discourse markers

risk avoidance strategies

These phenomena are frequently observed in Part 2.

3. Working Memory Models (Baddeley, 2000; Miyake & Friedman)

Part 2 engages multiple WM subsystems:

Phonological loop

holds and rehearses phrases before articulation

Visuo-spatial sketchpad

may assist imagery (e.g., when describing locations or events)

Central executive

allocates attention among planning, retrieval, and monitoring

Recent extensions (e.g., updating, shifting, inhibition) map neatly onto the demands of Part 2.

4. Robinson vs. Skehan (Task Complexity Frameworks)

Two influential SLA frameworks address performance under cognitive load:

Robinson’s Cognition Hypothesis

Predicts that increasing cognitive complexity:

increases accuracy and complexity

may reduce fluency

IELTS Part 2 increases complexity across:

reasoning

narrative sequencing

detail elaboration

Skehan’s Trade-Off Hypothesis

Predicts trade-offs between:

fluency vs. accuracy vs. complexity

In Part 2:

most candidates prioritize fluency + lexical variety

accuracy often drops under time pressure

IELTS raters observe these trade-offs directly.

5. Narrative Cognition & Event Schemas (Schank & Abelson; Bruner)

Narratives are cognitively economical because they rely on schemas like:

setting

initiating event

goal

outcome

evaluation

IELTS prompt cards exploit this by cueing schema-compatible topics:

“Describe a trip…”

“Describe a person…”

“Describe an event…”

This reduces conceptual load and shifts variation toward linguistic ability rather than world knowledge.

6. Lexical Access Models (Dell, Levelt again)

Extended monologue triggers:

lemma retrieval

lexeme selection

morphological encoding

L2 lexical retrieval is slower and more error-prone, producing:

filled pauses

self-repair

reformulation

circumlocution

IELTS examiners interpret these phenomena under the rubric category of fluency & coherence.

7. Monitoring Models (Levelt & Vygotskian Views)

The monitor checks output for:

grammaticality

appropriateness

pronunciation

coherence

task relevance

Under time constraints, monitoring becomes costly. Empirically:

high monitoring -> increased pauses

low monitoring -> increased errors

Part 2 forces a delicate balance.

8. Affective and Anxiety Models (Eysenck, MacIntyre & Gardner)

Performance conditions activate:

public self-awareness

evaluation anxiety

speech anxiety

According to Processing Efficiency Theory, anxiety selectively attacks working memory, especially:

updating

phonological rehearsal

This helps explain why fluent conversationalists sometimes struggle in Part 2.

9. Final Psycholinguistic Synthesis of Part 2

IELTS Speaking Part 2 can be conceptualized as:

a semi-planned L2 narrative task
requiring simultaneous operation of:

conceptualization (intent + schema)

formulation (lexical + syntactic + morphological encoding)

articulation (motor speech)

executive control (WM + inhibition + monitoring)

narrative cognition (event sequencing + evaluation)

affect regulation (public self-awareness)

time management (strategic allocation)

with trade-offs predicted by task complexity theory.

1. Shared Underlying Cognitive–Discourse Functions

Both Part 3 and Writing Task 2 require:

• abstraction (beyond personal narrative)
• opinion/stance formation
• argumentation or justification
• evaluative judgment
• comparison and contrast
• cause–effect / problem–solution reasoning
• consideration of alternatives

These align with what is typically called academic reasoning or decontextualized discourse.

2. Shared Pragmatic and Communicative Functions

Both tasks simulate the communicative behavior of an educated adult engaging with contemporary issues. The pragmatic frame is:

• analytical
• reflective
• socially aware
• non-episodic
• non-narrative (for the most part)

This is why many Task 2 topics reappear as Part 3 themes (education, media, work, environment, society, etc.).

3. Shared Construct in Assessment Terms

In assessment theory, both evaluate:

• coherence (logical organization)
• cohesion (linking mechanisms)
• lexical range (especially abstraction vocabulary)
• ability to support claims with reasons
• generalizations beyond self

These are hallmarks of academic/professional proficiency.

4. Differences You Should Not Ignore

Despite the similarities, they are not equivalent tasks. Key distinctions:

A. Planning Constraints

Writing Task 2:
• extended planning + drafting + revising (even if minimal under time pressure)

Speaking Part 3:
• zero explicit planning
• fully online processing
• self-monitoring under time pressure

This shifts the cognitive bottleneck from conceptual to production level.

B. Discourse Mode

Writing Task 2:
• monologic
• linear
• globally structured
• revision possible

Speaking Part 3:
• dialogic
• reactive
• interviewer-driven structure
• no revision (only repair)

This alters the organization of argumentation.

C. Lexico-grammatical Range

Writing allows (and often requires) more syntactic elaboration. Speaking typically favors syntactic compression (more parataxis, less subordination) unless candidate is highly proficient.

D. Cognitive Load Distribution

Speaking Part 3 recruits real-time:

• working memory
• conceptualization
• monitoring
• turn-taking management

Task 2 offloads considerable monitoring to spatial formatting (paragraphs, pauses, rereading).

E. Affective vs. Strategic Demands

Part 3 is susceptible to:

• performative anxiety
• interpersonal pragmatic signaling

Writing Task 2 is susceptible to:

• rhetorical strategy choice
• macro-structural misalignment

5. Strongest Theoretical Link

In psycholinguistic terms:

Part 3 approximates spoken argumentation
Task 2 approximates written argumentation

In assessment terms:

Part 3 tests real-time analytical discourse
Task 2 tests planned analytical discourse

In academic discourse theory:

Both fall within expository–argumentative macro-genres, just different modalities.
