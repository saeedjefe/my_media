Access to potable water has  been a major concern for humankind since the dawn of civilization. 
To address the issue, the water supply system, whose primary purpose is a continuous supply  of clean drinking
water, has evolved. However, this purpose has not always been completely fulfilled due to factors both within
and outside the system boundary. In fact, while it is true that conservation plays a major role in
ensuring a constant water supply, the argument that this measure is the best strategy seems to be an
oversimplification of a highly complex problem.	

In fact, the provision of potable water consists of several processes, each of which requires equal
attention. First, water resources --whether surface water, groundwater, or water collected from
the atmosphere-- near settlements must be identified. In the era of ancient Romans, the next step
was to convey water through iconic bridges to ornate fountains, where it was distributed among city
dwellers. At the time, it was only physical pollutants, such as soil or dirt, that must be
filtered, which was done through lead-containing pipes and sand channels. After the industrial revolution,
nevertheless, sophisticated farming practices and industrial development caused major concern over
other types of pollutants, primarily chemical ones. With herbicides, pesticides, and factory
discharges entering water resources, physical filtering was not effective anymore.
