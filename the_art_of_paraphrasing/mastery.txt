In the first part of Mastery, titled "The Ultimate Power," Robert Greene introduces an innate potential that exists
within all of us and argues that “it is the source of the greatest achievements and discoveries in history.”
However, in the absence of tension, this “form of power and intelligence” remains inaccessible. Once activated,
it profoundly affects our mental state during goal-oriented tasks as well as our social dynamics, with other
people seeming less resistant to us.

What keeps us from this unusual state of consciousness, he claims, is the fact that we often "live in an interior
world of dreams, desires, and obsessive thoughts." But the need to achieve something with practical consequences
might impel us to enter "a period of exceptional creativity."

However, as this period of "exceptional creativity" is transient, the experience might seem elusive or mysterious.
Therefore, he places a particular emphasis on the importance of unraveling the mystery.

Greene challenges the conventional wisdom that brilliance and creativity are the result of innate talent or
exceptional circumstances, arguing that this feeling could be systematically analyzed, manufactured and even
maintained. 

He then coins the term "mastery," defining it as "the feeling that we have a greater command of reality, people,
and ourselves." This feeling, he claims, is possessed by "masters." 

He subsequently maintains that a simple process, leading to "this feeling," is accessible to all of us, explaining
the process of learning a piano and entering a new job situation as illustrations.
 
Despite the initial excitement, transient emotions such as boredom, fear, or impatience may impede the process,
ultimately bringing it to a complete halt. By contrast, the effective management of such emotional states, coupled
with sustained persistence, typically culminates in the acquisition of the skills and self-confidence necessary for
the process to be sustained.
  
According to Greene, the sustained progression of the process subsequently results in the transition "from student
to practitioner," a turning point at which individuality begins to assert itself through the expansion of knowledge
and the incorporation of immediate feedback.

Finally, he refers to the previous illustrations to provide a metaphorical description of the final
stage, "mastery." In fact, he states that the piano keyboard becomes "part of our nervous system, our fingertips,"
while in the workplace, "a feel for the group dynamic" leads to a deeper insight into other people and the 
anticipation of their reactions. Having drawn such an analogy, he claims that so deep is our understanding of the
rules that "we can now be the ones to break or rewrite them."
 
He then presents a temporal framework for “the process,” with its first, second, and final stages labelled

Apprenticeship, Creative Active, and Mastery, respectively. To provide a metaphorical description, the author refers
to machinery whose parts are unfamiliar to the apprentice, who is portrayed as an outsider. In the next phase, not
only the parts but also the connections are plainly visible to the advanced learner, who is one step away from
Mastery. Masters, on the other hand, can see the big picture due to their “degree of knowledge, experience, and
focus.”

After this metaphorical explanation of the process, based on temporal progression, Greene brings up the idea that
this power could be considered intuition, which he defines as “a sudden and immediate seizing of what is real,
without the need for words and formulas.” This conceptualization of intuition, Greene believes, could reveal the
missing pieces to us.

He then compares an animal's learning process to that of humans, stating that the former largely depends on
intuition and the latter on thinking and rationality. According to Greene, this way of learning disconnects humans 
from the world. More importantly, he claims that "intuitive powers at the mastery level are a mix of the
instinctive and the rational, the conscious and the unconscious, the human and the animal." He also believes that
although we possess such powers during childhood, they are "generally drummed out of us by" information overload 
as we grow older. By contrast, Greene argues that masters "return to this childlike state," albeit with "degrees of
spontaneity and access to the unconscious."

Interestingly, Greene proposes that at the end of the three-step process is where we can gain
consistent access to this form of power, which we might have had glimpses of but could not hold on to. Indeed,
the fact that the world honors creativity and the unravelling of "new aspects of reality" results in the 
attainment of "practical tremendous power" in various social settings.
  
Throughout history, in their pursuit of  alchemy , people have practiced mystical rituals, taking drugs,
chanting incantations, and falling into trances so that they could reach that level of
consciousness and sense of control.  

Greene believes that the hunger for a "simple formula for success" could be traced back to these
practices. He states that although "there is a grain of truth and practicality on all of these
efforts," they have led to the stereotype of "the effortless path to practical power."

However, all the modern comforts, which we may take for granted, are the result of "the material effects" of such
power throughout history, claims Greene.  

In fact, Greene argues that the imaginary wall people have created around this power is called genius, a term that
makes us believe that it is not possessed by everyone. What contradicts this assumption, however, is that the human
brain is the result of six million years of development.
